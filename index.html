2 · On the Hub

Very easy tasks come first.

The CEFL Tech Practice Hub starts where a complete beginner starts: one small task at a time, in the language of the lectures. Each brief is written to be read slowly, and the words in bold are the terms to learn.

  • Nine Python tasks for weeks 2 to 4, then web practice and an AI task
  • No account and nothing to install: it runs in any browser
  • A word bank and hints on every task

Week 2 · The Mars rover

Three lefts make a right

The rover only knows how to turn left. Students write a new function, turn_right(), out of three turns to the left, and the rover delivers its rock.

It is the first big idea of programming, and it suits anyone who has been told they are behind: build what you need from what you already have.

Week 2 · Loops and if

Find the bottle

All the Python tasks on the Hub, including this one, run on a small Python interpreter I built for teaching. Because I built it myself, I can decide what students see when something goes wrong. Instead of Python’s own error messages, which are hard to read on day one, students first get feedback written for them: positive, and pointing to the next step rather than at the mistake. Python’s real messages come in gradually, as the students are ready for them.

Weeks 6 to 8 · Web development

Working in an international team

Students get a link to a small page, customised for them, that introduces how an international team of engineers works. They learn the language of that teamwork, and real skills with it: talking to the team through remote tools such as Slack, using a project board, and sharing files through GitHub.

The page is a robotics club’s, and it links to the University’s own Shorts from Computer Science: Go2, the robot dog (a YouTube Short, opens in a new tab) and Nao, the humanoid robot (a YouTube Short, opens in a new tab). Students see where the club’s ideas lead.

3 · How we teach

Words first. Code the same week.

Our foundation tech students start from zero: no computing concepts, and no code. This is how a week works for them now.

  1. Session 1 · Classroom The words of the week, such as input, variable and function, taught on paper. No computers.
  2. Session 2 · Computer room The same words used in real code on the Tech Practice Hub: robots first, then Python, then the web. We are now adding AI, in very simple tasks.
  3. Session 3 · Classroom Students explain what they did, and write a short reflection on Padlet after each session.
  4. Week 7 · A Year 1 lecture The group joins a Year 1 Computer Science lecture on object-oriented programming. Thank you to the home foundation team in Computer Science for arranging it, and to the lecturers who welcomed us. It was a great experience.

Week 2 · The first robot task

Wordy on purpose

For students who have English as a second language, the brief is long on purpose: before they write any code, they read five terms in the language of the lecture, again and again. I customise every element of the Hub, so I can shape its instructions, its tasks and the feedback it gives.

Then the rover does exactly what is written, in that order, and never guesses what you meant: move(), turn_left(), pick_rock() and put_rock(). The grey lines are locked, so a beginner cannot break the part that starts the program.

Week 3 · The first program

One line, and the words appear

We start with computational thinking: robot tasks with Python-style commands such as move() and turn_left(). By week 3, or sooner, students move on to real Python, and it makes sense to them, because they have practised its shape already.

The first program has one line. Run shows its words, and Run the tests checks them letter by letter.

The same idea at home

The smart fridge

A smart fridge looks high-tech, but its code is close to our students’ first programs: keep a number, compare it, and decide what to print. In week 3 that program is “Pass or fail?”.

milk_left = 1

if milk_left < 2:
    print("Milk is on the list.")
else:
    print("Enough milk.")

Everyone knows the moment the milk or the eggs run out. A familiar context makes new code easy to understand, and the same principle works with a local one: our towns, our buses, our shops.

4 · Access

Built for the learner who finds it hardest.

  • No sign-up, no account. Nothing to fill in, and nobody watching.
  • Progress kept on the laptop. Stop at any point, and carry on later.
  • A word bank on every task. Each new term explained where it is used.
  • Hints one at a time. Help without giving the answer away.
  • A robot you can slow down. Watch each step happen.
  • Works from the keyboard. Code boxes and buttons, with Tab, Escape and Enter.
  • Checklists that turn green. Progress you can see as you type.
  • Contrast checked. The text on eleven pages measured against WCAG AA.

5 · Why policing

Two numbers. One gap.

At the Accelerating AI conference in Milton Keynes, a police officer told us about a gap. Two numbers show it.

  • More than halfof the crime people in England and Wales experience is now fraud or computer misuse.
  • 2%of police funding goes on tackling fraud.

Sources: Crime Survey for England and Wales, year ending June 2026 (fraud and computer misuse: 5.3 million of 9.7 million incidents); House of Commons Justice Committee, Fraud and the Justice System (2022).

The gap

Part of it is funding, and a university can’t change that. The other part is people: not enough people with tech skills come into this line of work. That part, we can tackle.

Who

Adults who rule themselves out before they ever contact us. Above all, people who think they are too old to change career, and people who learn differently, including people with additional needs.

How

What holds them back is rarely ability. It is two things: the missing basics, with the words for them, and what they believe about themselves. In CEFL, we tackle both first, in small steps, with lots of guided practice.

The first step should not take courage. It should take ten minutes.

That is why we chose policing as our example. There is a clear need for cyber security officers in our own towns, and many of the people who could fill those roles are already here. What is often missing is a first step that feels possible.

11 · A customised path: cyber security

One day, five towns. Every case has a screen.

This is how we could customise a path for one audience, such as students heading for cyber security. Each made-up case, built from a common kind of crime, shows where computer science meets the job, and points to a module the Computer Science degree already teaches.

  1. 1 05:40 · Luton

    Parking that never existed

    A family arrives for an early flight. The airport parking they paid for online does not exist. The booking site looked professional, with reviews and a padlock in the address bar.

    The computer science: how a web address finds a server, what the padlock does and does not prove, and how to find out when a site was set up.

    Taught in: Hub, week 7 · BSc module Web Technologies and Platforms

  2. 2 09:15 · Northampton

    One car, two places

    A number-plate camera on the M1 flags a car near Northampton. The owner’s own doorbell camera shows the same car outside his house all morning.

    The computer science: how a camera turns a picture into text, why the same plate can appear on two cars, and how to check one record against another.

    Taught in: BSc modules Concepts and Technologies of Artificial Intelligence · Databases and Computer Networks

  3. 3 14:10 · Milton Keynes

    Locked out of every file

    A small firm off one of the grid roads opens its laptops after lunch. Every file is locked, and every screen asks for payment.

    The computer science: networks, backups and logs. How the program got in, where it spread, and what the logs can prove.

    Taught in: BSc modules Information Security · Databases and Computer Networks

  4. 4 16:30 · Dunstable

    “It’s your bank calling”

    A retired teacher gets a call. The number on her screen is her bank’s real number, and the caller knows her last two purchases.

    The computer science: how a caller’s number can be faked, and how leaked data makes a scam sound real.

    Taught in: BSc modules Information Security · Databases and Computer Networks

  5. 5 22:15 · Bedford

    The bike that never came

    Someone pays for a second-hand bike on a selling app. The seller disappears: three accounts, three names, one phone number.

    The computer science: databases. A query that finds the one detail different records share.

    Taught in: BSc module Systems Development and Modern Database Practices

What could come next: each case could become a full activity page, one for Luton, one for Northampton and so on, with direct links to the courses you can study at the University of Bedfordshire and to an expression-of-interest form.

15 · Questions colleagues ask

Questions colleagues may ask.

Is this a new course?

No. The first step already exists: the CEFL Tech Practice Hub, built and used by CEFL. Sharing it with the home foundation team in Computer Science, for their students who need an extra-slow start or have additional needs, has already been agreed with them: they are waiting for us to finish testing it with our own students first. The short courses are possibilities for discussion.

Have we talked to any police force?

No. The page names no force and claims no partnership. Policing is here as one example of the many local groups we could reach. Any contact would go through the right University team first.

What would a pilot cost?

Hosting the Hub is free, and the site keeps no personal data: each visitor’s progress stays in their own browser. Our GitHub organisation is also verified until November 2028, which adds an extra layer of protection: students’ nicknames, for example, stay hidden. A pilot, such as a short bootcamp, would need staff time, a computer room for a few sessions, someone to look after the people who come, and cover for normal teaching while a colleague runs it.

Why computer science?

I’m responsible for the academic skills unit on the EU/IFY Foundation Tech path, so it was a natural choice for me to show this as an example. Computer science also gives the fundamentals for many related degrees, from cyber security and software engineering to data and AI. The cases need all of these: data, networks, the web and an understanding of AI.

Our example focuses on the idea of recruiting local police officers to cyber security courses, but our tools could easily be adapted for business courses and other paths across the University. If your team would like to work with us, our contact details are at the bottom of this page.

What has been tested?

The Hub, with one EU Foundation group this semester. The policing part of this page, and its cases, have not been shown to anyone in policing.
